Overview

In this homework, I built a real-time cloth simulator using a mass-spring system. The cloth is represented as a grid of point masses connected by three types of springs: structural, shearing, and bending. I implemented numerical integration using Verlet integration to simulate the cloth's motion over time. I also added collision handling with spheres and planes and implemented self-collision detection using spatial hashing to prevent the cloth from clipping through itself. Finally, I wrote several GLSL shader programs to render the cloth with different lighting and material effects including diffuse, Blinn-Phong, texture mapping, bump mapping, displacement mapping, and environment-mapped reflections.

The most interesting thing I learned was how spatial hashing makes self-collision detection feasible in real time — instead of checking every pair of point masses (O(n²)), we only check masses that share the same 3D grid cell. I also found it fascinating how tweaking parameters like spring stiffness, density, and damping can dramatically change the cloth's behavior, from loose and flowing to stiff and rigid. Implementing the shaders was also a great way to see how different lighting models and texture effects can enhance the visual realism of the cloth.

Part 1: Masses and springs

To build the cloth grid, I first created an evenly spaced grid of point masses in row-major order. For a horizontal cloth, all point masses share y=1 and vary over the xz plane. For a vertical cloth, positions vary over the xy plane with a small random z offset. After placing all masses, I added three types of springs: structural springs connect each mass to the one directly to its left and directly above it; shearing springs connect each mass diagonally to its upper-left and upper-right neighbors; and bending springs connect each mass to the one two positions to its left and two positions above it.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

To simulate the cloth, I first computed the total force on each point mass by summing external forces (like gravity, applied uniformly using F=ma) and spring correction forces from Hooke's law. I then used Verlet integration to update each point mass's position based on its current position, previous position (which encodes velocity), and acceleration. A damping term slightly reduced the velocity each step to simulate energy loss. Finally, I applied a constraint that prevents any spring from stretching more than 10% beyond its rest length in a single timestep.

Effect of spring constant (ks): With a very low ks (e.g. 50), the cloth is very loose and stretchy — it droops heavily and takes longer to settle, with many small ripples. With a very high ks (e.g. 50000), the cloth is stiff and barely deforms, hanging almost flat between the pinned corners with very little sag.

Effect of density: Lower density means the cloth is lighter, so gravity has less effect — it hangs higher and sags less. Higher density makes the cloth heavier, causing it to sag more dramatically under gravity.

Effect of damping: With 0% damping, the cloth swings back and forth indefinitely, never settling — there is no energy loss so it oscillates forever. With high damping (1%), the cloth moves very slowly and smoothly, settling to rest quickly without any oscillation.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

For sphere collisions, I first checked if a point mass's position was inside the sphere by comparing its distance to the sphere's origin against the radius. If it was inside, I computed the tangent point. The tangent point is the point on the sphere's surface along the ray from the origin through the point mass. Then I corrected the point mass's position to that tangent point scaled by friction. For plane collisions, I checked if a point mass had crossed from one side of the plane to the other between its last and current position. This was done using the dot product with the plane normal. If it had crossed, I computed the intersection point and bumped the point mass back to just above the surface on the side it came from.

Effect of ks on sphere draping: With low ks (500), the cloth is very soft and drapes loosely around the sphere. With default ks (5000), it drapes naturally. With high ks (50000), the cloth is stiff and barely conforms to the sphere's shape, staying more spread out on top.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

To handle self-collisions efficiently, I implemented spatial hashing. At each timestep, I partitioned 3D space into boxes of size w×h×t and built a hash map from each box's hash value to the list of point masses inside it. To check for self-collisions, each point mass only needed to look up candidates in its own hash bucket. If two point masses were within 2×thickness of each other, I applied a correction vector to push them apart. The final correction is averaged over all colliding candidates and scaled down by the number of simulation steps for stability.

Effect of density on self-collision: With low density, the cloth is light and falls slowly, forming large gentle folds. With high density, the cloth is heavy and falls faster, crumpling into many smaller, tighter folds.

Effect of ks on self-collision: With low ks, the cloth is very flexible and folds into many small wrinkles. With high ks, the cloth is stiff and resists folding, forming fewer but larger, smoother folds.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a small program that runs on the GPU to determine the appearance of rendered geometry. Vertex shaders run once per vertex and handle geometric transformations — they take in per-vertex attributes like position and normal, transform them into screen space, and pass interpolated values to the fragment shader. Fragment shaders run once per pixel after rasterization and use the interpolated values from the vertex shader to compute the final pixel color. Together, they allow real-time rendering of complex lighting and material effects that would be far too slow to compute on the CPU.

The Blinn-Phong shading model combines three components: ambient light (a constant base illumination to prevent fully dark areas), diffuse reflection (light scattered equally in all directions, dependent on the angle between the surface normal and the light direction), and specular reflection (a shiny highlight dependent on the halfway vector between the light and camera directions). Together these produce a convincing approximation of real-world surface lighting.

Bump mapping modifies the surface normals based on a height map texture to give the illusion of surface detail without actually changing the geometry. Displacement mapping goes further by actually moving vertex positions along the normal direction based on the height map, creating real geometric detail visible on the silhouette of the mesh.

At low resolution (-o 16 -a 16), bump mapping looks identical to high resolution since it doesn't change actual geometry. Displacement mapping at low resolution looks very blocky and jagged on the silhouette since there are few vertices to displace. At high resolution (-o 128 -a 128), displacement mapping produces much smoother and more detailed geometric bumps since there are far more vertices available to be displaced.
